What Makes a Requirements Document Actually Useful

On the gap between a requirements document that exists and one that does its job — and what distinguishes the two.

Most projects have requirements documents. Very few have requirements documents anyone reads after the kickoff meeting. The rest become reference artifacts — things you point to in retrospect to establish who said what, rather than tools that actively shape how the work gets done.

The difference, in my experience, isn't in the format. It's in who the document is written for.

A document written for the project manager covers the right sections: scope, assumptions, constraints, acceptance criteria. A document written for the people who will actually implement the requirement says something different: not just what is needed, but why the current situation doesn't work, who is affected by it, and what good looks like from their point of view. That last part is the one that gets cut when time is short.

I've read templates — in Confluence, in Jira, in more proprietary tools than I want to count — that had every section a methodology could ask for, and still managed to say almost nothing. The fields were filled. The context was absent. When something went wrong in implementation, the document couldn't help, because it had documented the requirement without documenting the problem it was solving.

The frameworks exist. BABOK — the Business Analysis Body of Knowledge — is a thorough reference for what requirements analysis can cover. But a framework tells you what boxes to fill in. It doesn't tell you what to put in them. The gap is always in the prose, not the structure.

The most useful requirement I've ever written was two paragraphs. It described a situation — a B2B sales team re-entering the same order information in three places because their systems didn't talk to each other — and then described what would have to be true for that situation to no longer exist. No UML. No formal notation. Just enough specificity that anyone reading it could picture the problem and the resolution.

That's not always possible. Complex integrations need structured specs. Data migrations need field-by-field mapping tables. But the principle holds: the requirement that travels furthest through a project is the one that carries its own reasoning — the one that doesn't need its author present to be understood.
