From Expert to Teacher: Structuring a Language Course That Holds

Redesigning a Spanish beginner course from a body of expert knowledge into structured, teachable content — with explicit progression, defined entry points, and outcomes that can actually be measured.

Collaboration with a freelance Spanish teacher. Details anonymised. I worked on this because I saw the exact same problem I see in ERP: expert knowledge that works in one-on-one moments but falls apart when you try to scale it. The techniques here (learner profiles, objective mapping, prerequisites) work for language, ERP, or anything else.

The starting problem

The teacher had years of experience and materials that worked. Students left individual sessions with real progress. But the course as a whole had no architecture: each lesson was built as a standalone session, not as one step in a defined progression. There was no concept map, no entry diagnostic, and no explicit statement of what a student would be able to do at the end of each module.

The practical consequence: a student who missed week three couldn't follow week four, but neither the teacher nor the student could explain exactly why. The dependency was there — it just wasn't visible. And for a recorded course or a larger group, invisible dependencies are unscalable.

There was a second problem at the front door: students enrolled as "complete beginners" arrived with wildly different starting points. Someone who studied Spanish in secondary school and "remembers nothing" is actually a false beginner — they have implicit knowledge they don't know they have. Treating them as a zero-start learner wastes their time and the teacher's.

My hypothesis

If the course had an explicit architecture — learner profiles defined, learning objectives stated per lesson, prerequisites mapped, and a short entry diagnostic to route students to the right starting point — the teacher's expertise would become teachable to anyone, not just to the students who happened to ask the right questions.

Objective audit and prerequisite mapping

I worked through the existing lesson materials and extracted the implicit learning objectives — the things the teacher was clearly building toward, but hadn't written down. For each concept, I mapped what prior knowledge it assumed. Several "week one" items turned out to require concepts that were introduced in week three. The proposed architecture

Entry diagnostic

A five-question self-assessment at sign-up, covering: recognition of basic vocabulary, comprehension of a short spoken sentence, and one written production prompt. Scoring routes students to one of three entry points: Zero Start, False Beginner, or Elementary+. The diagnostic takes under three minutes and removes the first fifteen minutes of every first lesson that the teacher previously spent calibrating.

Module structure

Lesson template

Each lesson follows the same six-part structure, regardless of level or topic. The structure is visible to the student at the start of every session — so they always know where they are and what's coming next:

  • Hook (2 min) — a real-world scenario: "You're at a café in Madrid. The waiter asks you something. You'll understand this by the end of the lesson."
  • Core concept (8–10 min) — one new structure, introduced in context.
  • Worked examples (5 min) — the teacher models the structure with three variations.
  • Guided practice (10 min) — the student uses the structure with scaffolding.
  • Free practice (10 min) — open application, minimal prompt.
  • Recap + bridge (3 min) — what was learned, and what next week builds on it.

Bridge cards

Each lesson opens with a one-paragraph "bridge card" — a plain-language reminder of what was covered in the lesson this one depends on. Students who missed a session can read it and understand what they need to catch up on. Students who attended are reminded of the connection. The bridge card makes the invisible dependency visible.

One design decision explained

The teacher's natural instinct — shaped by years of experience — was to open beginner lessons with the grammar rule first: "In Spanish, every noun has a gender." Clear, accurate, and immediately alienating for a learner whose goal is to order a coffee, not pass a linguistics exam.

The decision was to restructure the first three lessons around a concrete scenario each, and surface the grammar rule as the explanation for something the student had already said successfully. The learner orders coffee. It works. Then they learn why it works — which reframes grammar from a barrier to an insight. The rule comes after the experience, not before.

This is the same principle I apply in ERP implementations: don't start with the system rules. Start with the outcome the user is trying to reach, and let the system logic follow from there.

How to measure success

  • Lesson completion rate per module — do students finish what they start?
  • Pre/post assessment delta — how much did measurable knowledge increase per module?
  • "I understood the objective of this lesson" self-rating (1–5) at the end of each session.
  • Module-to-module re-enrollment rate — do students who finish module 1 enroll in module 2?
  • Entry diagnostic accuracy — do students report the right difficulty level after week 2?

What I took away

Expert knowledge and teachable structure are not the same thing. The teacher had built incredible instincts over years — instincts about sequencing, pacing, when to intervene — but those instincts lived in the moment. They didn't exist on paper. My job was to make the invisible visible: extract what she did intuitively, write it down, sequence it so the structure holds even without her calibrating on the fly.

This is exactly what I do in ERP. An organization knows how work actually flows — the rules, the shortcuts, the exceptions. That knowledge is in people's heads. But once you capture it, structure it, map the dependencies, it becomes teachable. Portable. It works for anyone, not just the people who built it.
