Product concept

A Calendar That Tells You What Kind of Week You're Having

Every calendar looks the same — a grid of coloured blocks. This concept imposes a default structure by life category, so the week is readable at a glance without any extra effort.

Personal concept. Not affiliated with any existing product. This concept grew out of three years of managing overlapping work and personal calendars across two jobs and a daily commute — and consistently finding the week view useless for anything beyond knowing I was busy. Informed by daily use of Apple Calendar, Google Calendar, Sunsama and Fantastical, and conversations with colleagues who described the same pattern.

The user problem

Open any calendar app and you see a wall of coloured blocks — but the colours mean nothing consistent. A blue block might be a dentist appointment in one calendar, a work meeting in another. Over time you accumulate dozens of calendars, each colour assigned more or less randomly, and the week view becomes noise rather than signal.

Worse: managing which calendars are visible requires toggling each one individually. If you want to show "everything personal" or "everything related to travel" before a trip, you click through a long list one by one.

The result is a calendar that tells you when things happen, but not what kind of week you're building. Is this week heavy on commuting? Am I spending enough time outside? Is everything pro and nothing personal? The grid can't answer that.

My hypothesis

If a calendar app shipped with a default colour structure by life category — and let users group their calendars into those categories — the week view would become meaningful at a glance, without requiring any new behaviour from the user. The structure does the work; the user just looks.

The proposed structure

Default colour categories

Every calendar belongs to one of five categories. The app assigns a default colour to each; the user can override it, but there is always a "Reset to default" option:

Calendar folder groups

Instead of toggling calendars one by one, the app lets you group them into folders and toggle the entire folder at once. One tap on "All travel" hides commute calendars, ride-share bookings, and hotel confirmations simultaneously. One tap on "Personal only" removes everything work-related from the view.

Folders can be created and named freely. They can also belong to a colour category — so a "Travel" folder inherits the Commute red, and a "Side projects" folder inherits Work purple, unless overridden.

The default is the differentiator

Most calendar apps let you assign any colour to any calendar — which means users end up with arbitrary choices they never revisit. This concept flips it: the default structure is the product's opinion. Commute is red because it costs you something. Outside is blue because it opens up. Work is purple because it's distinct. To-do is green because it moves forward.

Users can override everything — colours, category assignments, folder membership — but the defaults are opinionated on purpose. The goal is a week view that is legible before you've done anything.

Why existing apps don't solve this

  • Apple Calendar / Google Calendar — any colour, any calendar. Zero default structure; the user does all the work.
  • Fantastical — best-in-class UI, but still colour = calendar name, no semantic layer.
  • Sunsama — close in spirit, but task-manager framing, not calendar-first. No mobile-first design.
  • Structured — beautiful daily view, no week-level category balance.

The gap: a calendar that has a point of view about what a week is made of, ships that structure by default, and gets out of the way once the user's week is loaded.

How to measure success

  • Weekly retention rate (do users return to plan the following week?).
  • Rate of default colour overrides (are users fighting the structure, or accepting it?).
  • Time-to-first-useful-view after account setup (how long before the week looks meaningful?).
  • Reported legibility score: "I can tell at a glance what kind of week this is" (1–5 CSAT).

Broader vision: a productivity suite

The calendar concept doesn't stand alone. Knowing what kind of week you're having is only useful if you can also control how you work within it. The natural next layer is a focus session tool — a Pomodoro-style timer that is aware of your calendar categories.

Concretely: when you open a Work block in the calendar, the app could offer a 25-minute focus session directly tied to that event. The timer knows it's a Work block (purple), tracks how many focus sessions you completed in that category today, and feeds back into the weekly balance bar. You'd see not just that you had 6 hours of "Work" scheduled — but that you actually focused for 4 of them.

The analogy I keep coming back to is ERP: the calendar is the planning layer (what should happen), the focus tool is the execution layer (what actually happened), and the balance bar is the reporting layer (how the week actually looked vs. how it was planned). Three modules, one coherent data model — the same logic that makes integrated ERP more useful than three disconnected spreadsheets.

This is a concept direction, not a current scope. The calendar module is the right starting point: if the structure isn't useful at the planning stage, adding a timer doesn't help.

Limits and next steps

The main risk is that the default categories don't fit everyone's life — a freelancer's week has no "commute", a student's has no "work" in the usual sense. The category set may need to be configurable at onboarding, with the default as a starting point rather than a fixed map.

The folder toggle is powerful but needs careful UX: toggling off "Work" should not silently hide a critical reminder. An "always visible" flag per event could protect non-negotiables.

Next steps: prototype in Figma — week view with balance bar, folder sidebar, colour grid editor. Test with 5 users who manage more than 3 calendars. Key question: does the default structure feel like help or constraint within the first 10 minutes?
